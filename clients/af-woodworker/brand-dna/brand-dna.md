## Voice

Five representative phrases from existing brand:
1. "Crafting custom cabinets" (site tagline)
2. "Modern, sleek, and fully customized cabinetry"
3. "High-quality work with the professionalism and expertise clients can trust"
4. "Precision CNC services, crafting custom props, decorative elements"
5. "Started as a passion project in a garage" (about page)

Common vocabulary: custom, modern, sleek, minimalist, tailor-made, craftsmanship,
precision, professionalism

Tone: calm, descriptive, craft-forward. Not salesy.

Technical vs conversational: leans slightly technical (engineering background
shows - "precision," "2D and 3D modeling") but not jargon-heavy

Traditional vs contemporary: clearly contemporary

Reserved vs expressive: reserved on the site (their own copy undersells them);
more expressive and specific in customer testimonials, which is the stronger
voice to actually pull from

Words/phrases to avoid: anything overtly luxury ("bespoke," "opulent"), heavy
urgency sales language, generic contractor boilerplate like the current services
page's "top-tier construction management and maintenance services" line - this
phrase does not sound like Ahmed and Fernando, it sounds like a template. Real
voice is closer to the about-page story and the customer testimonials.

## Physical Analogy

If AF Woodworker's brand were a physical object rather than a website, what
would it be?

Answer: **A woodworker's shop notebook / spec sheet** - precise, hand-drawn-feeling
but engineered, the kind of object that shows both the sketch and the math. Not
a glossy luxury lookbook (too far from their restrained, technical voice) and
not a big-manufacturer capabilities catalog (too corporate for a 2-founder
garage-to-workshop story that's only 3-4 years old).

Why: Their real differentiators are precision (CNC, 2D/3D modeling) and a
genuine hands-on founder story. A shop notebook captures both - technical
enough to reflect the engineering side, personal enough to reflect two named
founders who still do the work.

## Visual References

### Reference 1
What it is: Architectural spec/product sheets (the kind furniture makers or
fabricators use to spec a custom piece - material, dimension, finish callouts)
What's useful: The precision and confidence of showing real specs, not vague
marketing language - fits AF Woodworker's engineering-adjacent voice
What NOT to copy: The coldness/genericness of a pure spec sheet - needs the
warmth from their testimonials layered back in
How it translates: Project pages that show real process (the 2D/3D modeling
step they already do) as a visible part of the story, not hidden behind the
scenes

### Reference 2
What it is: A small-batch furniture maker's Instagram-to-website funnel (the
kind of maker who posts shop process content and sells through direct
relationship, not volume)
What's useful: Leaning into the founder-led, behind-the-scenes shop content
they already have (per Instagram highlights) but currently under-leveraged
What NOT to copy: The often-precious, overly artisanal tone some maker brands
use - AF Woodworker's voice is calmer and more professional than that
How it translates: Surface the workshop/process content that's already living
on Instagram (116 followers, low reach) onto the actual website where it can
be found and trusted

### Reference 3
What it is: A CNC/fabrication shop's capabilities page (not a cabinet company -
a metal or acrylic fabricator showing range of what their machine can do)
What's useful: AF Woodworker's CNC + acrylic + props capability is a genuine
differentiator from typical residential cabinet shops and is currently buried
under "Services" as one bullet among several
What NOT to copy: The industrial/commercial coldness of most fabrication-shop
sites - this needs to stay residential-warm, not shift into a B2B fabrication
brand
How it translates: A dedicated, well-photographed section for the CNC/custom
fabrication work, separate from standard cabinetry, so it reads as a real
second capability rather than a footnote
